# Dom Talks Politics: script style

**Channel promise:** boring, confusing US politics, explained like a smart friend would explain it, using
cartoons, plain words and the "here's why this matters to you" bit everyone else skips.
**Mix:** fast takes on trending stories (published within 24–48h) alongside evergreen explainers. Every
trending video is built on an evergreen idea, so it keeps getting views after the news moves on.
**Business goal:** maximise revenue, which means a broad US audience, long watch time, mid-roll-friendly
length and advertiser-safe tone.

---

## 1. What the reference scripts do (analysis of the five examples)

Source: the "Webber script examples" doc (Trump and the populist right, why Trump got bad at politics,
Dune and power, Farage and Count Binface, JD Vance's ideas). These are idea-led commentary videos, 12–25
minutes long and about 2,000–3,700 words, told in the first person.

| Technique | How they do it | Example from the doc |
|---|---|---|
| **Paradox hook** | Open on a puzzle: something that "shouldn't" be happening. | "There's lots of things you could say about Trump… but he's not someone you'd think of as dumb when it comes to politics. And yet now…" |
| **Clip collage cold open** | A rapid run of real quotes or soundbites, *then* the narrator steps in. | Quotes about affordability, "A-plus-plus-plus", data centres, all before the thesis. |
| **Direct address opener** | Talks straight to the viewer, like a friend starting a conversation. | "We need to talk about JD Vance." / "What is going on with Nigel Farage?" |
| **Misconception flip** | Names the common take, then says why it's wrong. | "Many have argued Vance is just a grifter without any real ideas, but I think this is a mistake." |
| **News peg → deeper idea** | The news is the way in; the payoff is a concept that outlives the headline. | "Three ideas that will be useful to you in understanding the news long after today's headlines have been forgotten." |
| **Explicit promise + roadmap** | Says what the video will give you, often a number. | "In this video I want to explain what's going on and share three ideas…" |
| **Define before you argue** | Pauses to explain a term, and what it *doesn't* mean. | "I'm not talking about liberals as in Democrats… I'm talking about philosophical liberalism." |
| **Numbered recap beats** | Closes each section with a one-line summary. | "So that's reason one: nationalism without liberal reciprocity falls apart…" |
| **Signposting** | Short spoken transitions that reset attention. | "Okay, let's do this." / "Okay, we've covered a lot." / "To explain the final way…" |
| **Absurd, concrete details** | Funny real specifics that make the point memorable. | Trump's speech about "a very inferior grass"; a candidate "dressed in a bin". |
| **Honest personal voice** | Admits mixed feelings and gives the other side credit before criticising. | "I have to admit I have kind of mixed feelings… I find him quite likable often." |
| **Steelman → critique** | Explains the other side's argument at its strongest, then tests it. | Vance's critique of loneliness is "not a mistake"… but his solution backfires. |
| **Quotes as evidence** | Their own words, short, then interpreted. | Vance: "Fire every single mid-level bureaucrat…" |
| **Caveat before the close** | Shows proportion, which builds trust. | "Of course, I don't want to overstate this…" |
| **Thesis callback ending** | The last line turns the opening paradox into a lesson. | "No, JD Vance, you were right the first time." |
| **End-screen handoff** | Sends viewers to the next specific video, explaining why it continues this one. | "The next video you should watch is all about how Trump broke the populist international right…" |

**What we copy:** the paradox hook, misconception flip, news-peg-to-idea structure, define-before-argue,
numbered recaps, signposting, absurd details, honest voice and the end-screen handoff.

**What we change:**
- **Not partisan.** The reference channel argues one side. For the widest US audience (and the most
  revenue), Dom is fair to both sides and has opinions about *how things work and what's true*, not about
  which team wins. He calls out misconceptions held by left *and* right.
- **No real clips or voice clones.** We can't play news footage or synthesise a real politician's voice.
  Quotes are short, read by Dom ("in his words…"), and shown on screen as a speech bubble from the
  character.
- **Shorter and simpler.** Target 8–12 minutes (1,300–1,900 words), written for someone who doesn't
  follow politics. The reference assumes a political-junkie audience.
- **Built for cartoons.** Every sentence must be something we can *show*.

## 2. Dom's voice

- **Explaining it to a friend over coffee.** Use "you", short sentences and everyday words. Never sound
  like a newsreader.
- **Curious, not cynical.** Treat politics as a puzzle we solve together, never as a team sport.
- **Honest.** Say "here's what we know, here's what we don't". Present allegations as allegations, and
  include the other side's response.
- **A bit of dry humour**, mostly from absurd real details, never from mocking ordinary people.
- **Dom has opinions about clarity and fairness.** For example, "this is a bad argument", "both sides get
  this wrong", "this is the bit nobody's talking about".

## 3. Required beats (every script)

| Beat | Purpose | Sounds like |
|---|---|---|
| **Paradox hook** (first 15s) | Stop the scroll with a puzzle or surprising fact. | "One bank collapsed, and a president lost his job. That's not supposed to happen." |
| **Promise** (by 30s) | Tell the viewer what they'll understand by the end. | "By the end of this you'll understand why… and the one thing that'll tell you what happens next." |
| **Why it matters** (≥3 times) | Connect each fact to the viewer's life, money or rights. | "This matters because…" / "Here's why you should care…" |
| **Pause and break it down** (≥2 times) | Slow down on the hard bit. | "Let's stop there for a second, because you need to get this bit." |
| **Misconception call-out** (≥1 time, ideally one per side) | Correct what people get wrong. | "You'll hear people say X. That's not quite right…" |
| **Define the jargon** (every new term) | No unexplained words. | "Impeachment, which basically means Congress puts the president on trial…" |
| **Story link** (between every part) | Each part answers a question the last one raised. | "So the money's in. Now watch what happens when people want it back." |
| **Recap beat** (end of each part) | One line so nobody gets lost. | "So that's the first piece: the bank grew too fast, with too much public money." |
| **Caveat** (near the end) | Proportion builds trust. | "Now, I don't want to overstate this…" |
| **Footnote close** | The one thing to remember. A channel signature. | "So, here's the footnote: …" |
| **Next-video handoff** | Sends the viewer to a specific next video. | "If you want to know why X, watch this one next, because…" |

## 4. Structure

```
COLD OPEN (0:00–0:30)  paradox hook, 1–3 short quotes or absurd details, then the promise
INTRO + TITLE          "Hi, I'm Dom, and this is Dom Talks Politics. Today's episode is about…"
PART 1..N (4–7 parts)  each one: story link in → facts → why it matters → break it down → recap
THE TWIST              the counter-intuitive turn ("Here's the twist…")
CAVEAT                 what we don't know / both sides
FOOTNOTE CLOSE         callback to the opening paradox, turned into a lesson
HANDOFF                next video + a one-line subscribe ask (no begging, no Patreon pitch)
```

**Trending vs evergreen:**
- **Trending (24–48h turnaround):** the news peg is the hook. At least one part must explain an evergreen
  concept (how impeachment works, what a filibuster is, why tariffs raise prices), so the video stays
  useful. Use "as we record this…" for anything still developing.
- **Evergreen:** the hook is a question people actually search ("What does the Speaker of the House
  actually do?"). Use a recent event as the example inside it.

## 5. Revenue rules

- **Length 8–12 min.** At 8 minutes or more, mid-rolls can run. End each part on an open question so a
  mid-roll at a part break doesn't lose the viewer.
- **US focus.** Use US stakes even for foreign stories ("why Washington cares", "what it means for your
  bank").
- **Advertiser-safe.** No swearing, no graphic violence, no slurs (even quoted), and no medical or
  financial advice. Facts with sources.
- **Titles and thumbnails** promise what the hook delivers, never more.

## 6. Writing for the voice and the visuals

- **Sentences:** average 12–16 words, none over 30. Read it aloud. If you'd need to breathe mid-sentence,
  split it.
- **Visuals:** one idea per sentence, and each sentence must be something we can draw.
- **Quotes:** at most 25 words. Introduce them ("In his words:"), then explain them. The pipeline turns
  them into `SAY()` bubbles.
- **Numbers:** round them ("about 3 million"), give one number per sentence, and always give it context
  ("that's 6 in every 10 adults").
- **Names:** say a full name and role the first time, then use the surname. New names go through
  `pronounce.py`.

## 7. Script file format (what the pipeline reads)

```
TITLE: One Bank Took Down a President
TYPE: trending            # or evergreen
TOPIC: Paraguay bank crisis
SOURCES:
- https://… (Reuters, 2026-11-05)
- …

[COLD OPEN]
One bank collapsed, and a president lost his job. …

[INTRO]
Hi, I'm Dom, and this is Dom Talks Politics. Today's episode is about Paraguay's bank crisis.

[PART 1 – The Bank]
…
QUOTE (Santiago Peña): "I was an investor. I'm an economist."
…
[TWIST]
[CAVEAT]
[FOOTNOTE]
[HANDOFF]
```
`script_check.py` lints the file: length, sentence length, required beats, quote length, part count,
sources, and banned or unsafe words. The pipeline won't move on to recording until it passes.
