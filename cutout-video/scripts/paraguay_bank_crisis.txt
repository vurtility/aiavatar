Hi, I'm Dom, and this is Dom Talks Politics. Today's episode is about Paraguay's bank crisis. In one week, a single bank collapse froze the savings of three million people and brought down a president. On Thursday, Paraguay's president Santiago Peña resigned in a video message, and his own Congress refused to let him go quietly. Peña is one of Donald Trump's closest partners in South America. Just two weeks earlier, he was meeting the US Treasury Secretary in Washington. So, how does a digital bank with cashback deals end up toppling a government? Let's follow the footnotes. Part 1. The Bank Paraguay is a landlocked country of about 6.5 million people wedged between Brazil, Argentina, and Bolivia. The bank at the center of this story is called ueno, spelt with a lower case U. Its roots go back to a finance company founded in 1976. In 2021, that company was bought by Grupo Vázquez, one of Paraguay's biggest business conglomerates. The new owners rebranded it as ueno, and went all in on an app. Reuters says it was compared to digital banks like New Bank, Monzo, and Revolut. At the end of 2023, a few months after Peña took office, the central bank authorized ueno to operate as a full bank. The central bank chief who approved it, Carlos Carvaggio, had previously been president of ueno's finance company. The central bank says Carvaggio recused himself from every decision about ueno. Critics have questioned that ever since. Part 2. The Boom ueno grew at astonishing speed. It lured customers with generous cashback on groceries and fuel up to 40% at partner stores. In 2024, it absorbed another lender, Visión Banco, adding more than a million customers in one move. It became a sponsor of Paraguay's national football team. Players banked there, and the bank's boss was a familiar face in the locker room. It raised money abroad too, including a $350 million bond on Wall Street, led by J.P. Morgan and Bank of America. By this year, ueno said it had about 3 million customers. That's roughly 60% of Paraguay's adults. For a lot of them, it was their first real bank account. Their salaries, their savings, their pensions, all on one phone app. Part 3. The Public Money ueno didn't just hold ordinary people's money. It also held a huge amount of the government's. In October, ueno's own president confirmed on the radio that the bank held more than $800 million in public deposits. Paraguay's economy ministry put the figure at around $850 million, including money from the giant Itaipú and Yacyretá dams. The biggest single chunk came from IPS, Paraguay's Social Security Institute. It had about $355 million at ueno. That's retirement money. The institute's finance chief said it was about 9% of its whole investment portfolio. She defended the deposits and said audits found no violations. But the institute had lowered its minimum risk score for banks. She says that was to diversify. One opposition senator called it the biggest swindle from political power of Paraguay's democratic era. That's his allegation and it hasn't been proven. Part 4. The President's Shares So where does the president come in? Santiago Peña is a 47-year-old economist. He once worked at the central bank and at the IMF. He was finance minister under former president Horacio Cartes, who called him a bright young man. In 2023, Peña won the presidency with 43% of the vote. Before that, Peña had invested in a credit house called GreenTro. It later folded into ueno's parent holding company. Peña says he held shares in Grupo Vázquez until March 2025, while he was already president and then sold them. His defense? I was an investor. I'm an economist. He says he never had any link to ueno Bank itself. When local reporters asked whether state and pension money was being steered to ueno, Peña called it a false media campaign. ueno also says Peña never had a direct relationship with the bank, as a shareholder or investor. Part 5 – The Collapse Over the past few months, regulators had been looking at ueno's lending, especially loans to companies tied to its owner, Grupo Vázquez. Then, in the early hours of Tuesday, November 3rd, the central bank put ueno into resolution. Its solvency had fallen below half the legal minimum. Every account was frozen. About 3 million customers suddenly couldn't touch their money. Pensioners who received state benefits through ueno were cut off. People lined up outside branches, asking for their savings. As the central bank rushed to move accounts to other banks, several banking apps across the country went down. The central bank chief, Karvaju, resigned the same day. A new chief was appointed. Even the national football team was hit. Ten players hired a lawyer who said the bank had lied to footballers, and the team released a letter demanding a forceful response from the government and the courts. Part 6 – The Arrests That same Tuesday, the central bank filed a 200-page criminal complaint, citing possible fraud and breach of trust. Prosecutors issued arrest warrants for eight ueno executives, including the bank's president and its CEO, Miguel Vázquez. Prosecutors suspect a scheme of risky loans and overdrafts to companies linked to the same business group. The case now covers 27 people. A judge ordered six of the executives held in pre-trial detention and banned them from even sharing a cell. Investigators have since seized a luxury yacht and a small plane belonging to Miguel Vázquez. They all remain presumed innocent. Congress rushed through a law to pay depositors back up to 40 monthly minimum wages each, starting within 24 hours. By Friday, the Senate's leader said more than 800,000 people had started getting money back. Millions were still waiting. Part 7 – The Streets Anger exploded. Thousands of people gathered outside Congress in Asunción, demanding that Peña answer for the collapse. On Thursday night, protesters pushed against police lines near Congress. Photos showed officers clashing with the crowd. Later, a group marched to a ueno office downtown and smashed its windows with rocks. A police officer was hit in the head and seven people were detained. Meanwhile, opposition lawmakers and members of Peña's own Colorado party filed an impeachment motion against him. They accused him of a grave and systematic failure to do his job, a conflict of interest and misuse of pension funds. Peña denies all of it. The Guardian reported, Peña had been ignoring warnings about the bank from the central bank and the press. Prosecutors are now investigating him, too. Part 8 – The Godfather The decisive blow came from inside his own party. Horacio Cartes, the former president who leads the Colorado party, was Peña's political mentor. On Thursday morning, Cartes turned on him. We will not stand on the side of the theft of public, private and pensioners funds. We support the impeachment of the president. With that, Peña's party abandoned him. Hours later, the lower house voted to impeach. All 78 deputies present voted yes, not a single no. While they were debating, Peña posted a video. I have decided to submit my resignation as president of the Republic. I want to apologize to the people if at any point I failed. It was never my intention to betray the trust you placed in me. Part 9 – Trump's Man To understand why this matters in Washington, look at how close Peña has been to Donald Trump. In January 2025, he flew to Washington for Trump's second inauguration. Bad weather moved it indoors and he didn't get a seat, but he kept showing up. The State Department called him a founding member of Trump's Board of Peace and his shield of the Americas. In June, Secretary of State Marco Rubio met him and praised what both sides call a strategic partnership. And on October 22, Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent hosted Peña to discuss the growing economic partnership between the two countries. Twelve days later, Peña's government was in freefall. Colombia's Elmpo called Paraguay one of the strongest bastions of Trump's network in Latin America. Part 10 – The Trial Here's the twist. Congress refused to accept Peña's resignation. And there's a reason. Under Paraguay's constitution, a president who resigns automatically becomes a senator for life with immunity from prosecution. If he's removed by impeachment instead, he loses that shield. So, when Congress met to vote on the resignation, his own party's deputies walked out and killed the quorum. Instead, the Senate turned itself into a court. Removing a president takes two-thirds, 30 of the 45 senators. On Friday morning, Peña asked for 72 more hours to prepare. He said he got the charges just the night before. This is not due process. The Senate said no. 39 votes to three. Peña didn't show up. His lawyers warned they could take the case to the Inter-American Court of Human Rights. Paraguay has done this before. In 2012, it removed President Fernando Lugo in about two days, and many neighbors called it a parliamentary coup. Peña's vice president, Pedro Alliana, is next in line. Critics suspect he's tied to the scandal, too. He denies it. And the lower house voted 48 to 30 not to impeach him. As we finish this video on Friday afternoon, the Senate is weighing the evidence. Its leader predicted a unanimous vote to convict. If Peña is removed, Alliana will be sworn in and serve out the term until August 2028. So, here's the footnote. A bank that promised to bring millions of Paraguayans into the system ended up locking them out of their own money. And a president who sat at the table with Trump's cabinet may now face the courts at home. Because in Paraguay, when your savings vanish, someone has to answer.
